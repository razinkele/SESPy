## Implications for SESPy

**CLD-based SES vulnerability in small-scale farming (key paper).** Chávez et al. apply causal loop modelling to quantify social-ecological vulnerability in small-scale farming contexts. The paper is behind a paywall so the specific method is not visible from the abstract, but the framing — CLDs as a tool to "unravel" SES vulnerability — is directly in SESPy's scope. The likely approach combines CLD structural analysis (feedback loops, centrality) with a vulnerability lens (exposure, sensitivity, adaptive capacity by node type or subgraph). This broadly validates SESPy's CLD + network analysis pipeline applied to agricultural SES. The cascade vulnerability indicator (issue #15) addresses the structural-resilience side; a complementary "exposure vs. adaptive capacity" vulnerability profile by DAPSI(W)R(M) node type could be a future extension, but without the method detail this is speculative. Actionability is limited to validation.

**Signed causal knowledge graphs and path polarity errors (methodological note for #16).** Hoehndorf et al. present PhysioMap — an ontology-grounded causal knowledge graph for physiology with typed signed edges and a "derivative signs" qualitative abstraction layer that handles steady-state analysis in the presence of feedback. Their key empirical finding is important for SESPy: *"Shortest signed paths produced directional errors, particularly on cases for which the PhysioMap solver did not determine a direction, indicating that its abstentions concentrated difficult cases."* In other words, when feedback loops are present, naively computing the compound polarity of a shortest path gives wrong answers; their solver correctly returns "indeterminate" when competing paths cancel. This is a direct methodological warning for the planned `causal_paths()` feature (issue #16): returning only compound path polarity without a determinacy flag misleads users when multiple paths of opposite polarity connect the same source–target pair. The fix — adding a `is_polarity_contested` flag based on whether all simple paths between source and target share the same sign — is an enhancement to issue #16 rather than a new issue, and should be noted there. No new issue warranted; link this finding to #16.

**Participatory dynamic modelling for WEFE nexus.** Mónico González et al. present a two-step framework: (1) stakeholder co-creation of a conceptual model, (2) translation of that model into a quantitative dynamic simulation (empirically validated against observed nexus performance) applied to the Segura River Basin. The participatory step 1 maps directly to SESPy's entry point (stakeholder co-developed CLD). Step 2 — quantitative simulation of climate scenarios and policy pathways — is well outside SESPy's qualitative scope. Broadly validates the participatory CLD-first workflow; no computational extension suggested.

**CLDs in undergraduate systems thinking education.** Sreenivasulu & Subramaniam study undergraduates' ability to construct CLDs when modelling global warming and transfer that skill to new contexts. Confirms that CLDs are accessible enough to be taught in undergraduate courses and transferred to novel domains. Validates SESPy's use of participatory CLDs as the entry point for complex system analysis by non-experts. No computational extension.
